## Programming exercises

**4.17** Implement the power iteration method to find the largest eigenvalue and the corresponding eigenvector of a symmetric matrix, and compare the result with np.linalg.eigh. What does the speed of convergence depend on?

```python
import numpy as np
 
def power_iteration(A, iters=200):
    x = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=A.shape[0])
    for _ in range(iters):
        x = A @ x
        x /= np.linalg.norm(x)               # keep unit length
    return x @ A @ x, x                      # Rayleigh quotient, vector
```

## Solutions

**Solution 4.1.** (a) $\det\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = 5$; the area is 5. (b) Along the first row: $2(12 - 0) - 0 + 1(1 - 0) = 25$. Along the second column: $- 0 + 3(8 - 0) - 1(0 - 1) = 25$. The volume is 25.

**Solution 4.2.** $\det\left( 2\mathbf{A} \right) = 2^{3} \cdot 3 = 24$ (every row is multiplied by 2); $\det\left( \mathbf{A}^{- 1} \right) = \frac{1}{3}$; $\det\left( \mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A} \right) = \det\left( \mathbf{A} \right)^{2} = 9$ (multiplicativity and $\det\mathbf{A}^{\top} = \det\mathbf{A}$); $\det\left( \mathbf{A}^{3} \right) = 27$. Swapping two rows changes the sign: $- 3$.

**Solution 4.3.** (a) $p(\lambda) = \lambda^{2} - 7\lambda + 6 = (\lambda - 6)(\lambda - 1)$. For $\lambda = 6$: $\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\lbrack 2,1\rbrack^{\top}$; for $\lambda = 1$: $\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\lbrack 1, - 2\rbrack^{\top}$. (b) $\lbrack 2,1\rbrack \cdot \lbrack 1, - 2\rbrack = 0$. (c) The total variance equals the trace of the matrix, $7$; so $\frac{6}{7} \approx 86\%$ of the variance lies along the first direction (a preview of PCA in Chapter 10).

**Solution 4.4.** $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1} + 3 \cdot \mathbf{1} = 4 \cdot \mathbf{1}$; hence $\lambda = 4$ with eigenspace $span\left\lbrack \mathbf{1} \right\rbrack$. For every $\mathbf{v}\bot\mathbf{1}$, $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$; hence $\lambda = 1$ with the two-dimensional eigenspace $\{\mathbf{v}:v_{1} + v_{2} + v_{3} = 0\} = span\left\lbrack \lbrack - 1,1,0\rbrack^{\top},\lbrack - 1,0,1\rbrack^{\top} \right\rbrack$. Check: the sum of the eigenvalues is $4 + 1 + 1 = 6 = tr\,\mathbf{A}$.

**Solution 4.5.** (a) Diagonal; invertible. (b) Symmetric and therefore diagonalisable, with eigenvalues 0 and 5; since zero is an eigenvalue, it is not invertible. (c) A 90-degree rotation; its eigenvalues are $\pm i$ and it is not diagonalisable over $\mathbb{R}$; but $\det = 1$ and it is invertible. (d) The only eigenvalue is 2, with algebraic multiplicity 2 and a one-dimensional eigenspace; it is not diagonalisable, but $\det = 4$ and it is invertible. Diagonalisability and invertibility are independent concepts.

**Solution 4.6.** The eigenvalues are 1 (vector $\lbrack 2,1\rbrack^{\top}$) and $0.7$ (vector $\lbrack - 1,1\rbrack^{\top}$). With $\mathbf{V} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & - 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ we have $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{V}\, diag(1,0.7)\,\mathbf{V}^{- 1}$ and

$$\mathbf{P}^{n} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 + {0.7}^{n} & 2 - 2 \cdot {0.7}^{n} \\ 1 - {0.7}^{n} & 1 + 2 \cdot {0.7}^{n} \end{bmatrix},\quad\quad\lim_{n \rightarrow \infty}\mathbf{P}^{n} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Every column of the limit is the stationary distribution $\left\lbrack \frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{3} \right\rbrack^{\top}$: whichever state we start from, the chain reaches that distribution; the speed of this convergence is determined by the second eigenvalue, $0.7$.

**Solution 4.7.** $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues 8 and 2 with eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_{1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\lbrack 1,1\rbrack^{\top}$ and $\mathbf{v}_{2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\lbrack 1, - 1\rbrack^{\top}$; hence $\sigma_{1} = 2\sqrt{2}$ and $\sigma_{2} = \sqrt{2}$. Then $\mathbf{u}_{i} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{i}/\sigma_{i}$ gives $\mathbf{u}_{1} = \lbrack 1,0\rbrack^{\top}$ and $\mathbf{u}_{2} = \lbrack 0,1\rbrack^{\top}$. Therefore

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2\sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 0 & \sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & - 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$

**Solution 4.8.** $\mathbf{A}_{1} = \sigma_{1}\mathbf{u}_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{A}_{1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & - 1 \end{bmatrix}$. The error is $\sigma_{2} = \sqrt{2}$ in the spectral norm and also $\sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt{2}$ in the Frobenius norm (because only one singular value has been discarded).

**Solution 4.9.** (a) If $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{1} = \lambda_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1}$ and $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{2} = \lambda_{2}\mathbf{v}_{2}$, then $\lambda_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{2} = \left( \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{1} \right)^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{2} = \mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{2} = \lambda_{2}\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{2}$; since $\lambda_{1} \neq \lambda_{2}$, $\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{2} = 0$. (b) If $\lambda = 0$, then $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$ would lie in the kernel of $\mathbf{A}$, contradicting invertibility. Multiplying both sides of $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$ by $\mathbf{A}^{- 1}$: $\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{A}^{- 1}\mathbf{v}$.

**Solution 4.10.** $l_{11} = 2$, $l_{21} = 1$, $l_{31} = 1$, $l_{22} = \sqrt{5 - 1} = 2$, $l_{32} = (3 - 1 \cdot 1)/2 = 1$ and $l_{33} = \sqrt{6 - 1 - 1} = 2$; hence $\mathbf{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, and the product $\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}$ gives back the original matrix.

**Solution 4.11.** The characteristic polynomial of $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$ is $\lambda^{2} - (a + d)\lambda + (ad - bc)$. On the other hand, $\left( \lambda - \lambda_{1} \right)\left( \lambda - \lambda_{2} \right) = \lambda^{2} - \left( \lambda_{1} + \lambda_{2} \right)\lambda + \lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}$; comparing coefficients, $\lambda_{1} + \lambda_{2} = tr$ and $\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2} = \det$. For (4.28): the trace is 7, the determinant is 6 and the eigenvalues are 1 and 6.

**Solution 4.12.** $\| \mathbf{A} \|_{2} = \sigma_{1} = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83$ and $\| \mathbf{A} \|_{F} = \sqrt{4 + 4 + 1 + 1} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$, which equals $\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2} + \sigma_{2}^{2}} = \sqrt{8 + 2}$. In general $\| \mathbf{A} \|_{F}^{2} = \sum_{i}^{}\sigma_{i}^{2} \geq \sigma_{1}^{2} = \| \mathbf{A} \|_{2}^{2}$.

**Solution 4.13.** A matrix with a negative determinant reverses the cyclic order of the vertices of the unit square. For example, $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, with determinant $- 1$, swaps the two axes: the area is preserved, but the direction in which the vertices are traversed changes from counter-clockwise to clockwise — like the mirror image of a shape.

**Solution 4.14.** The SVD is built from the eigendecomposition of the symmetric positive semidefinite matrix $\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$, which by the spectral theorem always exists; the eigendecomposition, however, needs $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors. The matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is not diagonalisable but has an SVD: its singular values are $\left( 1 + \sqrt{5} \right)/2 \approx 1.618$ and $\left( \sqrt{5} - 1 \right)/2 \approx 0.618$ — the golden ratio and its inverse.

**Solution 4.15.** "Energy" is the sum of the squared singular values, i.e., the squared Frobenius norm, and most of it comes from large-scale, smooth variations in the brightness of the image. Fine details such as edges and textures carry little energy but are very important to human perception; so a high energy fraction does not necessarily mean high visual quality.

**Solution 4.16.** The number of parameters is $k(m + n) = 50 \times \left( 10^{6} + 10^{4} \right) \approx 5.05 \times 10^{7}$, compared with $10^{10}$ entries in the full matrix — about half a percent. The low-rank assumption means that each user's taste and each movie's characteristics can be described by about 50 "latent factors".

**Solution 4.17.** Power iteration repeats $\mathbf{x}_{k + 1} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{k}/ \| \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{k} \|$. The error decreases at the rate $\left| \lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1} \right|^{k}$; so the larger the gap between the two largest eigenvalues, the faster the convergence. The complete code is in the book's repository.

**Solution 4.18.** $\mathbf{L} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 1.414 & 0 \\ 0.849 & 0.529 \end{bmatrix}$. With $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{z}$ and $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \mathbf{0},\mathbf{I} \right)$, the covariance $\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}$ is exactly the given matrix. The empirical covariance of 1000 samples typically differs from the theoretical value by a few hundredths, and this error decreases as $1/\sqrt{N}$.

**Solution 4.19.** The answer depends on the chosen image. Method: compute the singular values with np.linalg.svd, obtain the relative error from $\sqrt{\sum_{i > k}^{}\sigma_{i}^{2}/\sum_{i}^{}\sigma_{i}^{2}}$ for every $k$, and choose the smallest $k$ whose error is below the threshold. For natural images, $k$ is usually much smaller than the dimensions of the image.

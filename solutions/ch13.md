(c) For $\mathbf{z} = \left\lbrack 0,\,\ln 2,\,\ln 3 \right\rbrack^{\top}$ and the correct class being the third, find $\widehat{\mathbf{y}}$, the loss and the gradient.

**13.6** Compute the number of parameters of a network with 784 inputs (a $28 \times 28$ image), two hidden layers with 128 and 64 neurons, and 10 outputs. What percentage of the parameters is in the first layer?

**13.7** Under the assumptions of the text, prove (13.21). Then show that if $z_{\text{prev}}$ has a distribution symmetric about zero and $a = ReLU\left( z_{\text{prev}} \right)$, then $\mathbb{E}\left\lbrack a^{2} \right\rbrack = \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{V}\left\lbrack z_{\text{prev}} \right\rbrack$, and deduce He initialisation. ## Solutions

**Solution 13.1.** $z = 3 - 2 + 0.5 = 1.5$. Sigmoid: output $\sigma(1.5) \approx 0.818$ and derivative $0.818(1 - 0.818) \approx 0.149$. Hyperbolic tangent: output $\approx 0.905$ and derivative $1 - {0.905}^{2} \approx 0.181$. ReLU: output $1.5$ and derivative $1$.

**Solution 13.2.** With $\sigma = \left( 1 + e^{- z} \right)^{- 1}$ we have $\sigma^{\prime} = e^{- z}\left( 1 + e^{- z} \right)^{- 2} = \sigma(1 - \sigma)$, since $1 - \sigma = e^{- z}/\left( 1 + e^{- z} \right)$. For the hyperbolic tangent, by the quotient rule: $\tanh^{\prime} = \left\lbrack \left( e^{z} + e^{- z} \right)^{2} - \left( e^{z} - e^{- z} \right)^{2} \right\rbrack/\left( e^{z} + e^{- z} \right)^{2} = 1 - \tanh^{2}$. Also $2\sigma(2z) - 1 = \left( 1 - e^{- 2z} \right)/\left( 1 + e^{- 2z} \right) = \tanh(z)$. So the two functions are related by an affine transformation, and any tanh network can be turned into a sigmoid network with the same output by changing the weights and biases; their representational power is the same and the difference lies only in the training dynamics.

**Solution 13.3.** OR: $w_{1} = w_{2} = 1$ and $b = - 0.5$. AND: $w_{1} = w_{2} = 1$ and $b = - 1.5$. Since XOR is "OR and not AND", an output neuron with weight $+ 1$ on OR, weight $- 2$ on AND and bias $- 0.5$ works: for the four inputs, $z$ is $- 0.5$, $0.5$, $0.5$ and $1 - 2 - 0.5 = - 1.5$, respectively.

**Solution 13.4.** Forward: $\mathbf{z}^{(1)} = \lbrack 1 - 3,\, 0.5 + 1.5 - 1\rbrack^{\top} = \lbrack - 2,\, 1\rbrack^{\top}$, $\mathbf{a}^{(1)} = \lbrack 0,\, 1\rbrack^{\top}$, $z^{(2)} = - 2 - 1 = - 3$ and $\widehat{y} = \sigma( - 3) \approx 0.047$. Loss: $- \ln(1 - 0.047) \approx 0.049$. Backward: $\delta^{(2)} = 0.047$, $\partial L/\partial\mathbf{W}^{(2)} = \lbrack 0,\, 0.047\rbrack$, $\mathbf{\delta}^{(1)} = \left( \lbrack 2, - 2\rbrack^{\top} \cdot 0.047 \right) \odot \lbrack 0,1\rbrack^{\top} = \lbrack 0,\, - 0.095\rbrack^{\top}$ and $\partial L/\partial\mathbf{W}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\  - 0.095 & - 0.285 \end{bmatrix}$. The first hidden neuron is switched off; so the first row of $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$, the first bias and the output weight of that neuron have zero gradient. The prediction is already almost correct and all gradients are small.

**Solution 13.5.** (a) For $i = j$: $\partial{\widehat{y}}_{i}/\partial z_{i} = {\widehat{y}}_{i} - {\widehat{y}}_{i}^{2}$, and for $i \neq j$: $- {\widehat{y}}_{i}{\widehat{y}}_{j}$; both are summarised by ${\widehat{y}}_{i}\left( \delta_{ij} - {\widehat{y}}_{j} \right)$. (b) ${\widehat{y}}_{1} = e^{z_{1}}/\left( e^{z_{1}} + e^{z_{2}} \right) = \sigma\left( z_{1} - z_{2} \right)$; so with $z = z_{1} - z_{2}$ it is exactly the sigmoid, and (13.14) with $y_{2} = 1 - y_{1}$ becomes (13.12). (c) $\exp\left( \mathbf{z} \right) = \lbrack 1,2,3\rbrack$ and $\widehat{\mathbf{y}} = \left\lbrack \frac{1}{6},\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{2} \right\rbrack^{\top}$; the loss is $- \ln\frac{1}{2} \approx 0.693$ and the gradient is $\left\lbrack \frac{1}{6},\frac{1}{3}, - \frac{1}{2} \right\rbrack^{\top}$.

**Solution 13.6.** $(784 \cdot 128 + 128) + (128 \cdot 64 + 64) + (64 \cdot 10 + 10) = 100480 + 8256 + 650 = 109386$. The first layer holds about 92 percent of the parameters; this is why convolutional layers with shared weights are used for images.

**Solution 13.7.** Since the $w_{i}$ are independent of the $a_{i}$ and have zero mean, $\mathbb{V}\left\lbrack w_{i}a_{i} \right\rbrack = \mathbb{E}\left\lbrack w_{i}^{2} \right\rbrack\mathbb{E}\left\lbrack a_{i}^{2} \right\rbrack = \sigma_{w}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\lbrack a^{2} \right\rbrack$ and the terms are uncorrelated; summing $n$ terms gives (13.21). For ReLU and a symmetric distribution, $\mathbb{E}\left\lbrack a^{2} \right\rbrack = \mathbb{E}\left\lbrack z^{2}\mathbb{1}(z > 0) \right\rbrack = \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left\lbrack z^{2} \right\rbrack = \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{V}\left\lbrack z_{\text{prev}} \right\rbrack$. The condition $\mathbb{V}\lbrack z\rbrack = \mathbb{V}\left\lbrack z_{\text{prev}} \right\rbrack$ gives $n\sigma_{w}^{2}/2 = 1$, i.e., (13.23). For $n = 512$: $\sigma_{w} = \sqrt{2/512} = 0.0625$.

**Solution 13.8.** With equal weights, all neurons of a layer have identical $z$ and $a$; by (13.18) their errors, and by (13.19) the gradients of their weights, are also identical, and they stay identical after every step: the layer effectively has a single neuron. With zero weights the situation is even worse, because $\mathbf{\delta}^{(l)}$ also becomes zero. The biases can be zero because the random weights have already broken the symmetry.

**Solution 13.9.** Every factor in (13.24) contains $\sigma^{\prime} \leq \frac{1}{4}$; so the error of the first layer is shrunk, at best, by a factor of order ${0.25}^{9}$, and the steps of that layer are negligible. With ReLU, the derivative of active neurons is 1 and this attenuation does not occur. But if a neuron has $z < 0$ for all samples, its output and gradient are zero and it is never updated again (a "dead neuron"). A smaller learning rate and variants such as Leaky ReLU reduce this risk.

**Solution 13.10.** Similarity: both build a linear model in a feature space and give a nonlinear boundary in the input space. Differences: in the SVM the feature map is fixed and determined by the kernel, the problem is convex and the solution is unique, but the cost grows with the square to the cube of the number of samples. In a neural network the feature map is learned, the problem is nonconvex and the solution depends on the initialisation and the optimiser, but mini-batch training scales linearly with the number of samples.

**Solution 13.11.** With a Bernoulli mask, the expectation of $\mathbf{m} \odot \mathbf{a}$ is $(1 - p)\mathbf{a}$. Dividing by $1 - p$ restores this expectation to $\mathbf{a}$. If we do not do this, at test time (when all neurons are active) the input to each layer is on average $1/(1 - p)$ times larger than during training, and the predictions are distorted.

**Solution 13.12.** From epoch 30 onwards the network is overfitting: it is memorising patterns specific to the training data. Actions: early stopping and restoring the parameters from epoch 30; adding regularisation (weight decay or dropout); making the network smaller or adding data (including data augmentation).

**Solution 13.13.** The relative error $\| g_{\text{bp}} - g_{\text{num}} \| /\left( \| g_{\text{bp}} \| + \| g_{\text{num}} \| \right)$ with $\varepsilon = 10^{- 5}$ and double precision should be of order $10^{- 8}$ or smaller. With a deliberate bug (for example, removing the ReLU mask in the backward pass) this number rises to order $10^{- 1}$. Points that fall exactly on $z = 0$ of a ReLU neuron can cause small discrepancies.

**Solution 13.14.** Per epoch, a smaller batch makes faster progress because it takes more steps per epoch, but its curve is noisier. Per unit of running time, a very small batch is slower because of the loop overhead in Python, and a medium batch usually gives the best trade-off. The complete program is in the book's repository (ch13/minibatch.py).

**Solution 13.15.** With sigmoids, the gradient norm decreases by several orders of magnitude from the last layer to the first; with ReLU and He initialisation it stays roughly constant. The eight-layer sigmoid network makes almost no progress in the same number of epochs, whereas the ReLU network trains well.

**Solution 13.16.** Only two changes are needed: in the forward pass the last layer becomes a softmax (for numerical stability, subtract the maximum of each column before exponentiating) and the labels are one-hot encoded; the output error is still $\widehat{\mathbf{Y}} - \mathbf{Y}$. With a 64-64-32-10 network, standardised inputs and weight decay, a test accuracy of about $0.95$ to $0.97$, close to Listing 13.3, is obtained.

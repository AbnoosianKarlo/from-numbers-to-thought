## Conceptual exercises

**1.1** Assign each of the following problems to one of the four pillars of machine learning (regression, classification, dimensionality reduction or density estimation) and justify your choice: (a) predicting the price of an apartment; (b) detecting spam e-mail; (c) a two-dimensional display of face images for visualisation; (d) identifying unusual bank transactions.

**1.2** Explain why the degree of the polynomial in Figure 1.3 should not be chosen by minimising the test-set error and that same error then reported as an estimate of the generalisation error. What should be done instead?

**1.3** Name three everyday applications that benefit from deep learning and three for which classical models are sufficient. Explain the criterion you used to separate them.

**1.4** In Example 1.3, suppose that only 5% of the patients have the disease. Show that a model that always predicts "healthy" reaches an accuracy of 95%. Why is this model useless, and which metric exposes this weakness?

**1.5** A bank uses a model to approve loans. Of 500 applicants in group A, 200 are approved, and of 250 applicants in group B, 50 are approved. Compute the approval rate of each group and their ratio. According to the four-fifths rule (Example 1.4), does this model need to be reviewed? Give two possible causes of the difference.

**1.6** Using Sections 1.2 and 1.7, explain why old ideas such as neural networks, which had existed since the 1950s, achieved widespread success only after 2012.

## Mathematical exercises

**1.7** How many free parameters does a polynomial of degree M have? Show that for N points with distinct input values there is a polynomial of degree N−1 that passes through all of the points. (Hint: write down the resulting system of linear equations and see Chapter 2.)

**1.8** Consider the model $f(x) = \theta_{0} + \theta_{1}x$ and the three training points (0, 1), (1, 3) and (2, 4). Compute the mean-squared training error for θ₀ = 1 and θ₁ = 1.5. Can you find parameters with a smaller error?

**1.9** Three houses have the features A = (120, 3, 15), B = (80, 2, 5) and C = (150, 4, 30) (floor area, number of rooms, age). Compute the Euclidean distance between every pair of houses. Which feature dominates the distance, and why is this distance misleading without feature scaling? (A preview of Chapter 3.)

**1.10** Consider the model ŷ = 2x with training points (1, 2.5), (2, 3.5) and (3, 6.5) and test points (4, 6) and (5, 11). Compute the mean-squared training and test errors and the generalisation gap, and interpret the result.

## Programming exercises

**1.11** Extend Listing 1.2 to fit polynomials of degree 0 to 11. (a) Plot the training and test errors against the degree. (b) Repeat the experiment with 100 training points. How does the plot change, and why?

**1.12** Write a function train_test_split(X, y, ratio) that, without using ready-made libraries, randomly splits the data into a training part and a test part. Why must the data be shuffled before splitting?

**1.13** Plot the "learning curve" of a model: for training-set sizes of 10, 20, 50, 100 and 500 drawn from the data of Listing 1.1, fit a polynomial of degree 5 and plot the training and test errors against the size of the data. How do the two curves behave as the amount of data increases?

## Solutions

**Solution 1.1.** (a) Regression, because the output is a continuous number. (b) Classification, with two classes: spam and normal. (c) Dimensionality reduction, because the goal is to map high-dimensional data to two dimensions. (d) Density estimation; transactions that fall in low-density regions of the data distribution are unusual (Chapter 11).

**Solution 1.2.** If the test set is used for model selection, the selected degree is "fitted" to that same data and the reported error is an optimistic estimate of the generalisation error. The model should be selected with a separate validation set or with cross-validation, and the test set should be used only once, at the end (Chapter 8).

**Solution 1.3.** Sample answer: deep learning in unlocking a phone with one's face, voice assistants and machine translation; classical models in credit scoring, monthly sales forecasting and simple spam filtering. Criteria for the distinction: the type of data (perceptual, such as images and audio, versus tabular), the amount of data, and the need for interpretability.

**Solution 1.4.** Of 100 patients, 95 are healthy, so always predicting "healthy" is correct in 95 cases. But this model detects no patient at all and its recall is zero. Metrics such as recall, precision or F1 expose this weakness (Section 8.7).

**Solution 1.5.** The approval rate of group A is 200/500 = 0.4 and that of group B is 50/250 = 0.2; their ratio is 0.2/0.4 = 0.5, which is below 0.8, so the model needs to be reviewed. Possible causes: bias in the historical labels, and features that act as proxies for group membership, such as place of residence. A difference in rates does not by itself prove that discrimination exists; it is a signal that makes a closer investigation necessary.

**Solution 1.6.** The three factors of Section 1.2: far more data (such as ImageNet), the computing power of graphics processing units, and algorithmic and software improvements such as stochastic optimisation and automatic differentiation. Without these three, large neural networks could not be trained.

**Solution 1.7.** A polynomial of degree M has M+1 coefficients. For N points with distinct inputs, the condition of passing through all the points is a system of N equations in N unknowns for a polynomial of degree N−1, whose coefficient matrix — the Vandermonde matrix — is invertible; hence there is a unique solution.

**Solution 1.8.** The predictions are 1, 2.5 and 4 and the errors are 0, 0.5 and 0, so the training error is (0 + 0.25 + 0)/3 ≈ 0.083. Least squares (Chapter 9) gives θ₁ = 1.5 and θ₀ = 7/6 ≈ 1.17, whose error is 1/18 ≈ 0.056, which is smaller.

**Solution 1.9.** d(A,B) = √(1600 + 1 + 100) ≈ 41.2, d(A,C) = √(900 + 1 + 225) ≈ 33.6 and d(B,C) = √(4900 + 4 + 625) ≈ 74.4. Floor area, with a range of tens of square metres, dominates the distance and the number of rooms has almost no effect. After standardising each feature (Chapter 3), all features contribute in proportion.

**Solution 1.10.** The training predictions are 2, 4 and 6 and the errors are 0.5, −0.5 and 0.5, so the training error is 0.25. The test predictions are 8 and 10 and the errors are −2 and 1, so the test error is (4 + 1)/2 = 2.5 and the generalisation gap is 2.25. The model is good on the training data, but either it does not describe the true relationship well or the test data are very noisy; with two test points the two cases cannot be distinguished.

**Solution 1.11.** The training error decreases monotonically with the degree and becomes almost zero at degree 11, whereas the test error is U-shaped and its minimum usually occurs at degrees 3 to 5. With 100 training points overfitting is much weaker and the test error stays small for higher degrees as well, because more data prevent the model from fitting the noise.

**Solution 1.12.** Outline: build a random permutation of the indices (np.random.permutation), take the first ratio×n indices for training and the rest for testing. Shuffling is necessary because data are often ordered, for example by class or by time, and splitting without shuffling produces two parts with different distributions. The complete code is in the book's repository.

**Solution 1.13.** As the amount of data increases, the training error rises, the test error falls and the two curves approach each other. With little data the degree-5 model overfits; with a lot of data the generalisation gap becomes small and both errors approach the noise level of the data.

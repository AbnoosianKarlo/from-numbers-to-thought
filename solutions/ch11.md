## Programming exercises

**11.9** Implement the EM algorithm for a GMM from scratch and reproduce the result of the example of Figure 11.4. Print the log-likelihood at every iteration and check that it never decreases.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import multivariate_normal as mvn
 
def em_step(X, pi, mu, Sigma):
    K = len(pi)
    dens = np.column_stack([pi[k] * mvn.pdf(X, mu[k], Sigma[k]) for k in range(K)])
    R = dens / dens.sum(1, keepdims=True)             # E-step: responsibilities
    Nk = R.sum(0)
    mu = (R.T @ X) / Nk[:, None]                      # M-step
    Sigma = [((R[:, k, None] * (X - mu[k])).T @ (X - mu[k])) / Nk[k]
             + 1e-6 * np.eye(X.shape[1]) for k in range(K)]
    return Nk / len(X), mu, Sigma, np.log(dens.sum(1)).sum()
```

## Solutions

**Solution 11.1.** By Bayes' rule, $p\left( z_{nk} = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_{n} \right) = \frac{p\left( z_{nk} = 1 \right)\, p\left( \mathbf{x}_{n} \mid z_{nk} = 1 \right)}{\sum_{j}^{}p\left( z_{nj} = 1 \right)\, p\left( \mathbf{x}_{n} \mid z_{nj} = 1 \right)} = \frac{\pi_{k}\mathcal{N}\left( \mathbf{x}_{n} \mid \mathbf{\mu}_{k},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{k} \right)}{\sum_{j}^{}\pi_{j}\mathcal{N}\left( \mathbf{x}_{n} \mid \mathbf{\mu}_{j},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j} \right)} = r_{nk}$. Since the denominator is the sum of the numerators over all $k$, $\sum_{k}^{}r_{nk} = 1$.

**Solution 11.2.** With covariance $\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}$, $r_{nk} \propto \pi_{k}\exp\left( - \| \mathbf{x}_{n} - \mathbf{\mu}_{k} \|^{2}/2\sigma^{2} \right)$. As $\sigma^{2} \rightarrow 0$, the term of the nearest mean dominates all the others; hence $r_{nk} \rightarrow 1$ for the nearest component and zero for the rest. The M-step then sets each mean to the average of the points assigned to it — exactly the two steps of k-means.

**Solution 11.3.** If $\mu_{k} = x_{n}$, the term $\pi_{k}\mathcal{N}\left( x_{n} \mid x_{n},\sigma_{k}^{2} \right) = \pi_{k}/\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{k}^{2}}$ tends to infinity as $\sigma_{k} \rightarrow 0$, and since all the other terms are positive, the total log-likelihood becomes unbounded. Adding $\epsilon > 0$ to the variances — equivalent to a simple prior — bounds the variance from below and removes this singularity.

**Solution 11.4.** With diagonal covariances: $(K - 1) + KD + KD$ parameters (the weights, the means and the diagonal variances). With a full shared covariance: $(K - 1) + KD + D(D + 1)/2$. With separate full covariances the last term is $KD(D + 1)/2$, which grows quickly for large $D$ and requires much more data.

**Solution 11.5.** In customer segmentation, a customer may have characteristics of two groups. Hard clustering assigns them to a single group, whereas soft clustering gives the probability of membership in each group. In medical applications — for example, identifying the subtype of a disease — knowing the uncertainty of the assignment is essential, because borderline patients may need further tests.

**Solution 11.6.** The likelihood of the training data never decreases as $K$ increases, because a larger model can always mimic a smaller one. BIC, with a penalty proportional to the number of parameters and to $\log N$, and cross-validation, which measures the likelihood on unseen data, prevent overly complex models from being selected.

**Solution 11.7.** New transactions fall in a low-density region of the old model and are wrongly flagged as anomalous; that is, false alarms increase. Solution: update the model periodically or online with a sliding window of recent data, or give more weight to recent data.

**Solution 11.8.** The GMM log-likelihood is a nonconvex function of the parameters with many local maxima; EM is a local ascent method and reaches the maximum in whose basin of attraction the starting point lies. This is the same problem as the nonconvex optimisation of Chapter 7, with similar remedies: several runs with different starts and smart initialisation such as k-means++.

**Solution 11.9.** With the implementation of Listing 11.1, the log-likelihood increases or stays constant at every iteration: from $- 20.8$ to $- 15.5$ after one iteration and about $- 15.47$ after five iterations, and the final model is exactly (11.57).

**Solution 11.10.** The log-likelihood of the training data increases with $K$, but the BIC and the validation log-likelihood are both optimal at the true $K$, which is 3 for the data of Figure 11.15. On real data the two criteria sometimes disagree; BIC usually selects simpler models.

**Solution 11.11.** k-means, with its implicit assumption of spherical clusters of equal size, cuts elongated clusters apart and draws incorrect boundaries. A GMM with full covariances learns the elliptical shape of each cluster and identifies the clusters better — at the cost of more parameters and greater sensitivity to initialisation.

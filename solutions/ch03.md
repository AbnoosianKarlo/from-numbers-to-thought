## Mathematical exercises

**3.1** For which values of the real number $c$ is the expression $\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle: = x_{1}y_{1} + c\,\left( x_{1}y_{2} + x_{2}y_{1} \right) + 4x_{2}y_{2}$ an inner product on $\mathbb{R}^{2}$?

**3.2** On the space of polynomials of degree at most one, consider $\langle f,g\rangle: = \int_{0}^{1}f(x)g(x)\, dx$. (a) Show that this is an inner product. (b) Find $\langle 1,x\rangle$, $\| x \|$ and the angle between the polynomials $1$ and $x$.

**3.3** Two customers have feature vectors $\mathbf{x} = \lbrack 1,2,3\rbrack^{\top}$ and $\mathbf{y} = \lbrack 3,1,3\rbrack^{\top}$. Compute their distance using (a) the Euclidean norm; (b) the $\ell_{1}$ norm; (c) the $\ell_{\infty}$ norm; and (d) the weighted inner product $\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle = \mathbf{u}^{\top}diag(1,4,1)\mathbf{v}$. What does the weight 4 on the second feature mean?

**3.4** The word-count vectors of two documents are $\mathbf{x} = \lbrack 2,0,1,1\rbrack^{\top}$ and $\mathbf{y} = \lbrack 1,1,0,2\rbrack^{\top}$. (a) Compute their cosine similarity and the angle between them. (b) If the first document is repeated twice in a row ($2\mathbf{x}$), how do the cosine similarity and the Euclidean distance change?

**3.5** Project the vector $\mathbf{x} = \lbrack 2,3,1\rbrack^{\top}$ onto the line $U = span\left\lbrack \mathbf{b} \right\rbrack$ with $\mathbf{b} = \lbrack 1,1,1\rbrack^{\top}$. (a) Find $\pi_{U}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)$ and the projection matrix $\mathbf{P}$. (b) Compute the distance of $\mathbf{x}$ from $U$. (c) How is $\pi_{U}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)$ related to the mean of the components of $\mathbf{x}$?

**3.6** The subspace $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{3}$ is spanned by the columns of $\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Using the normal equations, find the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{x} = \lbrack 1,2,3\rbrack^{\top}$ onto $U$ and the distance of $\mathbf{x}$ from $U$, and check that the error vector is orthogonal to both columns of $\mathbf{B}$.

**3.7** For a nonzero vector $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$, consider the matrix $\mathbf{P} = \frac{\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^{\top}}{\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{b}}$. Show that (a) $\mathbf{P}$ is symmetric and $\mathbf{P}^{2} = \mathbf{P}$; (b) the rank of $\mathbf{P}$ is one; (c) the only possible eigenvalues of $\mathbf{P}$ are zero and one, and identify the corresponding eigenvectors.

**3.8** Use the Gram–Schmidt method to turn the vectors $\mathbf{b}_{1} = \lbrack 1,1,0\rbrack^{\top}$, $\mathbf{b}_{2} = \lbrack 1,0,1\rbrack^{\top}$ and $\mathbf{b}_{3} = \lbrack 0,1,1\rbrack^{\top}$ into an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^{3}$.

**3.9** (a) Use the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to show that the cosine similarity of any two nonzero vectors lies in $\lbrack - 1,1\rbrack$ and that equality $\left| \cos\omega \right| = 1$ holds only when the two vectors are collinear. (b) Prove the reverse triangle inequality: $|\, \| \mathbf{x} \| - \| \mathbf{y} \| \,| \leq \| \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} \|$.

**3.10** For a unit vector $\mathbf{u}$, consider the Householder reflection matrix $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{I} - 2\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{\top}$. (a) Show that $\mathbf{H}$ is symmetric and orthogonal and that $\mathbf{H}^{2} = \mathbf{I}$. (b) For $\mathbf{u} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\lbrack 1,1\rbrack^{\top}$, compute $\mathbf{H}$ and $\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}$ for $\mathbf{x} = \lbrack 3,1\rbrack^{\top}$, and show that the length of the vector is preserved.

## Conceptual exercises

**3.11** Explain why the unit ball of the ℓ₁ norm in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ is a diamond. By drawing an arbitrary line that touches the unit ball, show why the point of contact for ℓ₁ often lies on one of the axes, whereas for ℓ₂ it does not. How is this related to the sparsity of solutions in the Lasso?

**3.12** In a data set with two features, "height in metres" and "weight in grams", we find a person's nearest neighbour using the Euclidean distance. Which feature determines the decision? How does standardisation fix this problem?

**3.13** Why may the $k$-nearest-neighbour algorithm perform poorly in high dimensions? Suggest two ways to mitigate this problem.

**3.14** Show that if $\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L}$, then $d_{\mathbf{M}}\left( \mathbf{x},\mathbf{y} \right) = \| \mathbf{L}\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{L}\mathbf{y} \|$. Why must $\mathbf{M}$ be positive definite for $d_{\mathbf{M}}$ to be a metric?

## Programming exercises

**3.15** Write a function that computes the cosine similarity and the Euclidean distance between a query vector and all rows of a matrix in vectorised form (without loops). Use it to reproduce the results of Example 3.13.

```python
import numpy as np
 
def cosine_sim(q, X):
    Xn = X / np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)   # normalise rows
    return Xn @ (q / np.linalg.norm(q))
 
X = np.array([[1, 1, 0, 2], [2, 2, 0, 4], [0, 1, 3, 0]], float)
print(cosine_sim(X[0], X))                  # -> [1. 1. 0.13]
print(np.linalg.norm(X - X[0], axis=1))     # Euclidean distances
```

## Solutions

**Solution 3.1.** The expression equals $\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{y}$ with $\mathbf{M} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & c \\ c & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, which is bilinear and symmetric for every $c$. $\mathbf{M}$ must be positive definite: $x_{1}^{2} + 2cx_{1}x_{2} + 4x_{2}^{2} = \left( x_{1} + cx_{2} \right)^{2} + \left( 4 - c^{2} \right)x_{2}^{2} > 0$ for every $\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$ if and only if $4 - c^{2} > 0$, i.e., $|c| < 2$.

**Solution 3.2.** (a) Bilinearity and symmetry follow from the linearity of the integral; and $\langle f,f\rangle = \int_{0}^{1}f^{2}\, dx \geq 0$, with equality only if $f$ vanishes on $\lbrack 0,1\rbrack$, which for a polynomial means $f = 0$. (b) $\langle 1,x\rangle = \frac{1}{2}$, $\| 1 \| = 1$ and $\| x \| = \sqrt{\int_{0}^{1}x^{2}dx} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$; hence $\cos\omega = \frac{1/2}{1/\sqrt{3}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $\omega = 30^{\circ}$.

**Solution 3.3.** With $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} = \lbrack - 2,1,0\rbrack^{\top}$: (a) $\sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$; (b) $3$; (c) $2$; (d) $\sqrt{4 + 4 \cdot 1 + 0} = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83$. The weight 4 means that a difference in the second feature counts four times as much in the squared distance; in other words, the second feature has been scaled by a factor of two.

**Solution 3.4.** (a) $\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y} = 4$ and $\| \mathbf{x} \| = \| \mathbf{y} \| = \sqrt{6}$; hence $\cos\omega = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3}$ and $\omega \approx {48.2}^{\circ}$. (b) The cosine similarity does not change, because it does not depend on the lengths of the vectors; but the Euclidean distance grows from $\| \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} \| = \sqrt{4} = 2$ to $\| 2\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} \| = \sqrt{9 + 1 + 4 + 0} = \sqrt{14} \approx 3.74$. This is why cosine similarity is preferred in text retrieval.

**Solution 3.5.** (a) $\pi_{U}\left( \mathbf{x} \right) = \frac{\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{b} = \frac{6}{3}\mathbf{b} = \lbrack 2,2,2\rbrack^{\top}$ and $\mathbf{P} = \frac{1}{3}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}$ (all entries equal to $\frac{1}{3}$). (b) The error vector is $\lbrack 0,1, - 1\rbrack^{\top}$ and the distance is $\sqrt{2}$. (c) Every component of the projection equals the mean of the components of $\mathbf{x}$; projecting onto $\mathbf{1}$ is "replacing by the mean", and $\mathbf{x} - \pi_{U}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)$ is the centred data.

**Solution 3.6.** $\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{x} = \lbrack 3,5\rbrack^{\top}$; hence $\mathbf{\lambda} = \left( \mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B} \right)^{- 1}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{x} = \frac{1}{3}\lbrack 1,7\rbrack^{\top}$ and $\pi_{U}\left( \mathbf{x} \right) = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{\lambda} = \frac{1}{3}\lbrack 1,8,7\rbrack^{\top}$. The error vector is $\frac{1}{3}\lbrack 2, - 2,2\rbrack^{\top}$, whose inner products with $\lbrack 1,1,0\rbrack^{\top}$ and $\lbrack 0,1,1\rbrack^{\top}$ are zero. The distance is $\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{12} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 1.15$.

**Solution 3.7.** (a) $\mathbf{P}^{\top} = \mathbf{P}$ is clear, and $\mathbf{P}^{2} = \frac{\mathbf{b}\left( \mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{b} \right)\mathbf{b}^{\top}}{\left( \mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{b} \right)^{2}} = \mathbf{P}$. (b) Every column of $\mathbf{P}$ is a multiple of $\mathbf{b}$; hence $rk\left( \mathbf{P} \right) = 1$. (c) If $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$, then $\lambda\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{P}^{2}\mathbf{v} = \lambda^{2}\mathbf{v}$ and $\lambda \in \{ 0,1\}$. The vector $\mathbf{b}$ has eigenvalue 1, and every vector orthogonal to $\mathbf{b}$ has eigenvalue zero.

**Solution 3.8.** $\mathbf{q}_{1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\lbrack 1,1,0\rbrack^{\top}$. Next, $\mathbf{b}_{2} - \left( \mathbf{q}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{b}_{2} \right)\mathbf{q}_{1} = \left\lbrack \frac{1}{2}, - \frac{1}{2},1 \right\rbrack^{\top}$ and $\mathbf{q}_{2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\lbrack 1, - 1,2\rbrack^{\top}$. Finally, $\mathbf{b}_{3} - \left( \mathbf{q}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{b}_{3} \right)\mathbf{q}_{1} - \left( \mathbf{q}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{b}_{3} \right)\mathbf{q}_{2} = \left\lbrack - \frac{2}{3},\frac{2}{3},\frac{2}{3} \right\rbrack^{\top}$ and $\mathbf{q}_{3} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\lbrack - 1,1,1\rbrack^{\top}$. The result can be checked via $\mathbf{q}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{q}_{j} = \delta_{ij}$.

**Solution 3.9.** (a) Dividing $\left| \mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y} \right| \leq \| \mathbf{x} \| \| \mathbf{y} \|$ by $\| \mathbf{x} \| \| \mathbf{y} \|$ gives $\left| \cos\omega \right| \leq 1$. Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz holds when $\mathbf{x} - t\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$ for some $t$, i.e., when the vectors are collinear. (b) By the triangle inequality, $\| \mathbf{x} \| = \| \left( \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} \right) + \mathbf{y} \| \leq \| \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} \| + \| \mathbf{y} \|$, so $\| \mathbf{x} \| - \| \mathbf{y} \| \leq \| \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} \|$; swapping the roles of the two vectors gives the claim with the absolute value.

**Solution 3.10.** (a) Symmetry is clear and $\mathbf{H}^{2} = \mathbf{I} - 4\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{\top} + 4\mathbf{u}\left( \mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{u} \right)\mathbf{u}^{\top} = \mathbf{I}$; since $\mathbf{H}^{\top} = \mathbf{H}$, we have $\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{I}$. (b) $\mathbf{H} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & - 1 \\  - 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x} = \lbrack - 1, - 3\rbrack^{\top}$; both vectors have length $\sqrt{10}$. $\mathbf{H}$ reflects a vector across the line orthogonal to $\mathbf{u}$.

**Solution 3.11.** The ℓ₁ unit ball is the set $\left| x_{1} \right| + \left| x_{2} \right| = 1$, made up of four line segments between the points $( \pm 1,0)$ and $(0, \pm 1)$, i.e., a diamond. A line that touches this diamond is most likely to touch it at one of its corners, which lie on the axes — that is, at a solution with one component equal to zero. In the Lasso, this same geometry causes many coefficients to become exactly zero (Chapter 9).

**Solution 3.12.** Weight in grams takes values in the tens of thousands while height in metres is between 1 and 2; so the distance depends almost entirely on weight. Standardisation — subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation of each feature — gives both features a proportionate share of the distance.

**Solution 3.13.** In high dimensions distances concentrate (Section 3.10.2): the nearest and the farthest neighbour are almost equally far away, and the notion of "nearest" loses its meaning. Remedies: dimensionality reduction with PCA (Chapter 10) and learning a suitable metric (Section 3.10.3).

**Solution 3.14.** $d_{\mathbf{M}}\left( \mathbf{x},\mathbf{y} \right)^{2} = \left( \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} \right)^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L}\left( \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} \right) = \| \mathbf{L}\left( \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} \right) \|^{2}$. If $\mathbf{M}$ is not positive definite, there is a nonzero vector with $d_{\mathbf{M}} = 0$ or the squared distance becomes negative; in either case the metric axioms are violated.

**Solution 3.15.** For the first row, the code outputs the cosine similarities $\lbrack 1,1,0.13\rbrack$: the second row is twice the first and has cosine similarity 1, even though their Euclidean distance is large. This is the same difference we saw in Example 3.13.

**Solution 3.16.** As $D$ grows, the ratio (largest − smallest)/smallest tends to zero; for $D = 2$ it is usually larger than 10, and for $D = 1000$ about 0.1 or less. That is, in high dimensions all points are almost equally far apart. The complete code is in the book's repository.

**Solution 3.17.** Because rounding errors accumulate, the classical Gram–Schmidt implementation gradually loses orthogonality and $\| \mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q} - \mathbf{I} \|$ may reach $10^{- 8}$ or more; np.linalg.qr, which uses Householder reflections, achieves an error of about $10^{- 15}$. Modified Gram–Schmidt is much better than the classical version.

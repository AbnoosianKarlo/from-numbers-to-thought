**10.10** Reconstruct and plot a few digit images with $M = 2,5,10,21$ components. Plot the mean reconstruction error against $M$ and compare it with the sum of the discarded eigenvalues (equation (10.44)).

**10.11** Use power iteration (Exercise 4.17) to find the first principal component of the digits data, and then find the second component by subtracting the projection of the data onto this component (Section 10.2.2).

## Solutions

**Solution 10.1.** For every centred point, $\mathbf{x}_{n} = {\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}}_{n} + \left( \mathbf{x}_{n} - {\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}}_{n} \right)$, where ${\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}}_{n}$ is the orthogonal projection onto the principal subspace and the two terms are orthogonal. By Pythagoras, $\| \mathbf{x}_{n} \|^{2} = \| {\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}}_{n} \|^{2} + \| \mathbf{x}_{n} - {\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}}_{n} \|^{2}$, and averaging gives: total variance = retained variance + mean reconstruction error. Since the total variance is fixed, maximising the first term is equivalent to minimising the second.

**Solution 10.2.** If the rows of $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$ are the data points, the covariance matrix is $\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\mathbf{V}^{\top}$; hence the principal components are the leading columns of $\mathbf{V}$ and the eigenvalues are $\sigma_{i}^{2}/N$. The codes are $\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{V}_{M} = \mathbf{U}_{M}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{M}$ and the reconstruction is $\mathbf{U}_{M}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{M}\mathbf{V}_{M}^{\top}$ — exactly the Eckart–Young rank-$M$ approximation (Chapter 4).

**Solution 10.3.** After standardisation every feature has variance 1; so the $(i,j)$ entry of the covariance matrix of the standardised data, $Cov\left\lbrack x_{i},x_{j} \right\rbrack/\left( \sigma_{i}\sigma_{j} \right)$, is the correlation coefficient. Hence PCA is performed on the correlation matrix, and the result does not depend on the units in which the features are measured.

**Solution 10.4.** The eigenvalues are 4 and 2, with eigenvectors $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\lbrack 1,1\rbrack^{\top}$ and $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\lbrack 1, - 1\rbrack^{\top}$. The first component explains $4/(4 + 2) = 2/3 \approx 67\%$ of the variance.

**Solution 10.5.** PCA projects the data onto the two directions with the largest total variance, not onto the directions that best separate the digits; "5" and "8" may be similar along these two directions but different along others. Overlap in two dimensions does not imply inseparability in 64 dimensions; a classifier in the full space usually separates these two digits with high accuracy.

**Solution 10.6.** PCA is unsupervised and keeps the high-variance directions; if the two classes are separated only along a low-variance direction, that direction is discarded and the classes overlap in the projection. Supervised methods such as linear discriminant analysis (LDA) find the direction that maximises the ratio of the between-class variance to the within-class variance.

**Solution 10.7.** It means that the largest source of variation in the data is a technical "batch effect", not the biology of the samples. Before continuing, this effect must be removed with batch-correction methods or included as a variable in the model; otherwise subsequent results may merely reflect the day of the experiment.

**Solution 10.8.** PCA is a deterministic method and gives only the subspace and the codes. PPCA is a probabilistic generative model with Gaussian noise: it has a likelihood, so models can be compared and the number of components can be chosen; missing data can be handled with EM; new samples can be generated from it; and it can be used inside larger models, such as a mixture of PPCAs.

**Solution 10.9.** The implementation based on the SVD of the centred data gives the same components as scikit-learn. The signs of some components may differ, because if $\mathbf{b}$ is an eigenvector then so is $- \mathbf{b}$, and different algorithms use different sign conventions.

**Solution 10.10.** The mean reconstruction error decreases monotonically with $M$ and is exactly equal to the sum of the discarded eigenvalues. Images reconstructed with $M = 21$, which retains about 90% of the variance, are clearly recognisable.

**Solution 10.11.** Power iteration on the covariance matrix converges to the first component. After subtracting the projection of the data onto this component, the remaining covariance matrix has the first eigenvector with eigenvalue zero, and power iteration on it converges to the second component. The speed of convergence depends on the ratio of the two largest eigenvalues.

## Mathematical exercises

**6.1** The weather is denoted by $X \in \{ x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}$ (sunny, cloudy and rainy, respectively) and traffic by $Y \in \{ y_{1},y_{2}\}$ (light and heavy). The joint distribution is: $P\left( x_{1},y_{1} \right) = 0.30$, $P\left( x_{1},y_{2} \right) = 0.10$, $P\left( x_{2},y_{1} \right) = 0.15$, $P\left( x_{2},y_{2} \right) = 0.15$, $P\left( x_{3},y_{1} \right) = 0.05$ and $P\left( x_{3},y_{2} \right) = 0.25$. (a) Find the marginal distributions. (b) Compute $P\left( y_{2} \mid x_{3} \right)$ and $P\left( x_{3} \mid y_{2} \right)$ and state the meaning of each. (c) Are $X$ and $Y$ independent?

**6.2** The random variable $X$ follows the mixture $p(x) = 0.3\,\mathcal{N}(x \mid - 1,1) + 0.7\,\mathcal{N}(x \mid 3,0.5)$. Compute the mean and variance of $X$. Does the mean lie in a region of high density?

**6.3** 30% of the e-mails in a mailbox are spam. The word "free" appears in 60% of spam e-mails and in 5% of normal e-mails. If an e-mail contains this word, what is the probability that it is spam?

**6.4** The random variable $X$ takes the values 0, 1, 2 and 3 with probabilities 0.1, 0.3, 0.4 and 0.2, respectively. Compute $\mathbb{E}\lbrack X\rbrack$ and $\mathbb{V}\lbrack X\rbrack$ with the raw-score formula (6.44).

**6.5** If $\mathbb{V}\lbrack X\rbrack = 4$, $\mathbb{V}\lbrack Y\rbrack = 1$ and $Cov\lbrack X,Y\rbrack = - 1$, compute the variance of $Z = 2X - 3Y$.

**6.6** Take the prior $Beta(2,3)$ for the probability that a coin lands heads. After observing 7 heads in 10 tosses, find the posterior distribution, the posterior mean and the MAP estimate, and compare them with the maximum likelihood estimate.

**6.7** The random vector $\lbrack x,y\rbrack^{\top}$ has a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and covariance $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Compute the conditional distribution $p(x \mid y = 2)$ using (6.66) and (6.67).

**6.8** If $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{2} \right)$ and $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$ with $\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{b} = \lbrack 1, - 1\rbrack^{\top}$, find the distribution of $\mathbf{y}$.

**6.9** If $X$ is uniform on $(0,1)$, show that $Y = - \ln X$ has the exponential distribution with density $f(y) = e^{- y}$ for $y > 0$. How is this result related to the probability integral transform?

**6.10** Show that the Poisson distribution $p(x \mid \lambda) = \frac{\lambda^{x}e^{- \lambda}}{x!}$ belongs to the exponential family, and identify its natural parameter $\theta$, sufficient statistic $\phi(x)$ and log-partition function $A(\theta)$.

**6.11** Let $X$ be uniform on $\{ - 1,0,1\}$ and $Y = X^{2}$. Show that $X$ and $Y$ are uncorrelated but not independent.

**6.12** Compute the empirical mean and the empirical covariance matrix (6.42) of the four points $(1,2)$, $(3,4)$, $(5,0)$ and $(3,2)$.

**6.13** If $X \sim \mathcal{N}(1,4)$ and $Y \sim \mathcal{N}( - 2,9)$ are independent, find the distributions of $X + Y$ and $3X - Y$.

**6.18** Simulate Example 6.18: create a population of 100,000 people, randomly generate each person's disease status and test result, and compute the proportion of ill people among those who test positive. Compare the result with the analytical answer.

```python
import numpy as np
 
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

```

## Solutions

**Solution 6.1.** (a) $P(X) = \lbrack 0.4,\, 0.3,\, 0.3\rbrack$ and $P(Y) = \lbrack 0.5,\, 0.5\rbrack$. (b) $P\left( y_{2} \mid x_{3} \right) = \frac{0.25}{0.3} \approx 0.83$: on rainy days traffic is heavy with probability about 83%. $P\left( x_{3} \mid y_{2} \right) = \frac{0.25}{0.5} = 0.5$: half of the heavy-traffic days are rainy. The two conditional probabilities are not equal. (c) No; for example, $P\left( x_{1},y_{1} \right) = 0.30 \neq P\left( x_{1} \right)P\left( y_{1} \right) = 0.20$.

**Solution 6.2.** $\mathbb{E}\lbrack X\rbrack = 0.3( - 1) + 0.7(3) = 1.8$. With $\mathbb{E}\left\lbrack X^{2} \right\rbrack = \sum_{k}^{}\pi_{k}\left( \sigma_{k}^{2} + \mu_{k}^{2} \right) = 0.3(1 + 1) + 0.7(0.5 + 9) = 7.25$ we get $\mathbb{V}\lbrack X\rbrack = 7.25 - {1.8}^{2} = 4.01$. The mean 1.8 lies between the two peaks, in a region of low density; the mean is not a good representative of a multimodal distribution (Chapter 11).

**Solution 6.3.** $P(\text{free}) = 0.3 \cdot 0.6 + 0.7 \cdot 0.05 = 0.215$, and by Bayes' rule $P(\text{spam} \mid \text{free}) = 0.18/0.215 \approx 0.837$.

**Solution 6.4.** $\mathbb{E}\lbrack X\rbrack = 0.3 + 0.8 + 0.6 = 1.7$ and $\mathbb{E}\left\lbrack X^{2} \right\rbrack = 0.3 + 1.6 + 1.8 = 3.7$; hence $\mathbb{V}\lbrack X\rbrack = 3.7 - {1.7}^{2} = 0.81$.

**Solution 6.5.** $\mathbb{V}\lbrack 2X - 3Y\rbrack = 4\mathbb{V}\lbrack X\rbrack + 9\mathbb{V}\lbrack Y\rbrack - 12\, Cov\lbrack X,Y\rbrack = 16 + 9 + 12 = 37$.

**Solution 6.6.** The posterior is $Beta(2 + 7,3 + 3) = Beta(9,6)$. The posterior mean is $9/15 = 0.6$ and the MAP estimate is $(9 - 1)/(9 + 6 - 2) = 8/13 \approx 0.615$. The maximum likelihood estimate is $7/10 = 0.7$; the prior pulls the estimate towards the prior mean, 0.4.

**Solution 6.7.** $\mu_{x \mid y = 2} = 0 + \frac{1}{1}(2 - 0) = 2$ and $\sigma_{x \mid y}^{2} = 2 - \frac{1^{2}}{1} = 1$; hence $p(x \mid y = 2) = \mathcal{N}(2,1)$.

**Solution 6.8.** By (6.50) and (6.51), $\mathbf{y} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \mathbf{b},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top} \right) = \mathcal{N}\left( \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\  - 1 \end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \right)$.

**Solution 6.9.** $F_{Y}(y) = P\left( - \ln X \leq y \right) = P\left( X \geq e^{- y} \right) = 1 - e^{- y}$ for $y > 0$; hence $f(y) = e^{- y}$. This is the inverse-transform method: applying the inverse of the exponential cumulative distribution function to a uniform variable yields a sample from the exponential distribution.

**Solution 6.10.** $p(x \mid \lambda) = \frac{1}{x!}\exp\left( x\log\lambda - \lambda \right)$. Hence $h(x) = 1/x!$, $\theta = \log\lambda$, $\phi(x) = x$ and $A(\theta) = \lambda = e^{\theta}$.

**Solution 6.11.** $\mathbb{E}\lbrack X\rbrack = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}\lbrack XY\rbrack = \mathbb{E}\left\lbrack X^{3} \right\rbrack = 0$; hence $Cov\lbrack X,Y\rbrack = 0$. But $P(Y = 0 \mid X = 0) = 1 \neq P(Y = 0) = \frac{1}{3}$; so they are not independent. Correlation measures only linear dependence.

**Solution 6.12.** The mean is $\lbrack 3,2\rbrack^{\top}$. The deviations from the mean are $( - 2,0)$, $(0,2)$, $(2, - 2)$ and $(0,0)$; hence $\mathbf{\Sigma} = \frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix} 8 & - 4 \\  - 4 & 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & - 1 \\  - 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution 6.13.** $X + Y \sim \mathcal{N}(1 - 2,\, 4 + 9) = \mathcal{N}( - 1,13)$ and $3X - Y \sim \mathcal{N}(3 + 2,\, 9 \cdot 4 + 9) = \mathcal{N}(5,45)$.

**Solution 6.14.** With a prevalence of 20% and the same sensitivity and specificity as in Example 6.18, the posterior probability of being ill becomes much higher, because the ratio of true patients to false positives increases. Conclusion: the value of a positive test depends not only on the accuracy of the test but also on the prevalence of the disease in the tested population.

**Solution 6.15.** Example: Exercise 6.11. In feature selection, if we rely only on correlation, features with a strong nonlinear relationship to the target, such as $Y = X^{2}$, are discarded; measures such as mutual information do not have this problem.

**Solution 6.16.** Aleatoric uncertainty: the inherent noise of the sensors, for example lidar in the rain. Epistemic uncertainty: the behaviour of the model in a situation it has never seen, for example a snow-covered road for a model trained only on summer data. Only epistemic uncertainty decreases with more data.

**Solution 6.17.** With the prior $Beta(1,1)$, i.e., the uniform prior, the MAP estimate equals the maximum likelihood estimate exactly, because a uniform prior plays no role in the maximisation.

**Solution 6.18.** In the simulation, the proportion of ill people among the positive tests approaches the analytical posterior probability of Example 6.18, and its deviation shrinks as $1/\sqrt{N}$. The complete code is in the book's repository.

**Solution 6.19.** The mean and variance of the samples with $x_{2} \approx 1$ approach 0.4 and 0.32 respectively — the theoretical values (6.70) and (6.71); the narrower the window around $x_{2} = 1$ and the larger the number of samples, the better the agreement.

**Solution 6.20.** As the number of tosses increases, the posterior becomes narrower and its peak approaches 0.7; the width of the posterior shrinks roughly as $1/\sqrt{N}$ and the influence of the prior gradually vanishes.

## Mathematical exercises

**14.1** Three binary classifiers with AUCs of $0.92$, $0.85$ and $0.70$ have estimated a patient's probability of disease as $0.8$, $0.6$ and $0.2$. Compute the combined probability with (14.2) and with a simple average, and compare.

**14.2** For the data $2,\ 3,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 40$, compute the sample standard deviation and the robust estimator (14.5). How does each change if the value $40$ is changed to $400$?

**14.3** In the AVSD method with the parameters given in the text, obtain the adaptive percentile for entropy $H = 4.0$ and $H = 6.2$. In an image with two million voxels inside the brain mask, how many voxels become candidates in each case?

**14.4** (a) Compute the HOMA-IR index for a fasting blood glucose of $110$ mg/dL and an insulin level of $15$ µU/mL. (b) A program takes $12$ seconds when run sequentially and $4$ seconds on eight cores; find the speed-up and the efficiency.

**14.5** In FCM with $m = 2$ and two centres $c_{1} = 0.3$ and $c_{2} = 0.8$, compute the membership of a pixel with intensity $x = 0.6$ in each cluster. If $m$ is increased to $3$, do the memberships move closer to $0.5$ or further away?

**14.6** Consider an image with $200$-dimensional rows. Count the parameters of a Gaussian component with a full covariance and of a factor-analyser component with $q = 2$, and find their ratio.

## Conceptual exercises

**14.7** In the IPDD data with 837 diabetic, 103 healthy and 53 prediabetic samples, what accuracy does a classifier that declares everyone "diabetic" achieve? Why is this number misleading, and which metrics expose its weakness?

**14.8** Why must SMOTE and the computation of the standardisation parameters be carried out on the training part only? If they are done before splitting the data, in which direction is the error estimate biased?

**14.9** The model of Section 14.6 has an accuracy of $0.838$ on the Pima data and $0.585$ on the Bangladesh data, while its overall accuracy is reported as $0.814$. What do these three numbers say together, and what should be done before using the model in Bangladesh?

**14.10** In Table 14.2 the pixel accuracy of many methods is above 99 percent, yet their Dice scores range from 80 to 99 percent. Why is pixel accuracy not a suitable metric for tumour segmentation? (Hint: what fraction of the pixels of a slice does the tumour make up?)

**14.11** In model stacking, why must the meta-model be trained on out-of-fold predictions? Otherwise, what happens if one of the base models has completely memorised the training data?

## Solutions

**Solution 14.1.** Weighted combination: $(0.92 \cdot 0.8 + 0.85 \cdot 0.6 + 0.70 \cdot 0.2)/(0.92 + 0.85 + 0.70) = 1.386/2.47 \approx 0.561$. Simple average: $1.6/3 \approx 0.533$. Weighting has reduced the influence of the weaker model's vote (which gave a low probability) and moved the combined probability towards the opinion of the two stronger models.

**Solution 14.2.** The mean is $9.5$ and the sample standard deviation is about $14.98$. The median is $3.5$ and the absolute deviations are $1.5,\ 0.5,\ 0.5,\ 0.5,\ 1.5,\ 36.5$, whose median is $1.0$; hence ${\widehat{\sigma}}_{\text{rob}} = 1/0.6745 \approx 1.48$. When $40$ is changed to $400$, the ordinary standard deviation grows about tenfold, but the robust estimator does not change at all, because medians do not depend on the value of the most distant point.

**Solution 14.3.** For $H = 4.0$: $P = 99.7 - \frac{4.0 - 2.5}{3.0}(5.5) = 96.95$; so $3.05$ percent, i.e., about 61 thousand voxels, become candidates. For $H = 6.2$ the entropy is clipped to $5.5$ and $P = 94.2$; i.e., $5.8$ percent or 116 thousand voxels. In both cases more than 94 percent of the voxels are discarded before clustering.

**Solution 14.4.** (a) $110 \times 15/405 \approx 4.07$. (b) $S = 12/4 = 3$ and $E = 3/8 = 0.375$; that is, less than forty percent of the power of the eight cores has been turned into useful work.

**Solution 14.5.** The distances are $0.3$ and $0.2$. $u_{1} = 1/\left( 1 + (0.3/0.2)^{2} \right) = 1/3.25 \approx 0.308$ and $u_{2} \approx 0.692$. With $m = 3$ the exponent $2/(m - 1)$ drops from $2$ to $1$: $u_{1} = 1/(1 + 1.5) = 0.4$ and $u_{2} = 0.6$; the memberships move closer to $0.5$, i.e., become fuzzier.

**Solution 14.6.** Full Gaussian: a mean of $200$ and a covariance of $200 \times 201/2 = 20,100$, $20,300$ in total. Factor analyser: a mean of $200$, factor loadings of $400$ and a diagonal variance of $200$, $800$ in total (one parameter is redundant because of the rotational freedom). The ratio is about $25$.

**Solution 14.7.** The accuracy is $837/993 \approx 0.843$, without the model having learned anything. The recall of the other two classes is zero, the class-averaged F1 is very low and the AUC is $0.5$; these metrics reveal the weakness because they give each class equal weight or do not depend on a threshold.

**Solution 14.8.** A synthetic SMOTE sample is a linear combination of two real samples. If it is created before splitting, a training sample may have been built from a test sample, and the model has effectively seen the test data. The mean and standard deviation computed on the whole data set also carry information from the test set into training. In both cases the estimated error is lower than the true error, i.e., optimistic.

**Solution 14.9.** The overall accuracy is a weighted average of the performance on the two populations (after balancing), and because the Pima data set is larger, it dominates. The model is effectively good for the Pima population and only slightly better than random guessing for the Bangladeshi population; the paper itself attributes this drop to ethnic and diagnostic differences. Before use in Bangladesh, more data must be collected from that population, the model must be retrained or adapted, and its performance must be reported separately for that population.

**Solution 14.10.** In a typical slice the tumour may make up one to five percent of the pixels. An algorithm that declares no pixel to be tumour has an accuracy of 95 to 99 percent while doing nothing — the same imbalance problem as in Section 14.1. The Dice coefficient counts only the tumour pixels in the output and the reference, and the abundant background pixels have no effect on it; this is why it is the main metric for segmentation.

**Solution 14.11.** A model that has memorised the data has zero error on that data. If the meta-model is trained on in-sample predictions, it learns always to follow this model, because it apparently never makes a mistake; but on new data this very model generalises worst. Out-of-fold predictions show how each base model behaves on data it has not seen, and that is exactly what the meta-model needs to know.

## Mathematical exercises

**12.1** Consider the hyperplane $3x^{(1)} + 4x^{(2)} - 10 = 0$. (a) Find the labels of the points $\lbrack 4,\, 2\rbrack^{\top}$ and $\lbrack 1,\, 1\rbrack^{\top}$ and their distances to the hyperplane. (b) If the training point closest to the hyperplane has $\left| f\left( \mathbf{x} \right) \right| = 5$, write $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ in canonical scale and find the width of the margin.

**12.2** A training set has only two points: $\mathbf{x}_{-} = \lbrack 1,\, 0\rbrack^{\top}$ with label $- 1$ and $\mathbf{x}_{+} = \lbrack 3,\, 4\rbrack^{\top}$ with label $+ 1$. (a) Solve the hard-margin SVM and find $\mathbf{w}$, $b$ and the margin. (b) Show that for two points in general, $\mathbf{w} = 2\left( \mathbf{x}_{+} - \mathbf{x}_{-} \right)/ \| \mathbf{x}_{+} - \mathbf{x}_{-} \|^{2}$ and the decision boundary is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the two points.

**12.3** For the problem of Exercise 12.2, find the dual coefficients and show that strong duality holds by computing the value of the dual objective (12.41).

**12.4** Show that the hinge loss $\ell(t) = \max\{ 0,1 - t\}$ is convex, write its subderivative at every point (including $t = 1$), and deduce from it a subgradient of $\ell\left( y\,\left( \langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle + b \right) \right)$ with respect to $\mathbf{w}$.

**12.5** A soft-margin SVM with $C = 2$ has returned the solution $\mathbf{w} = \lbrack 1,\, - 1\rbrack^{\top}$ and $b = 0$. The training points are $\lbrack 2,\, 0\rbrack^{\top}$ and $\lbrack 0.5,\, 0\rbrack^{\top}$ with label $+ 1$ and $\lbrack 0,\, 1\rbrack^{\top}$ and $\lbrack 1,\, 0.5\rbrack^{\top}$ with label $- 1$. Compute the slack variables and the value of the objective. Which points are misclassified, and which can be support vectors?

**12.6** Show that the kernel $k\left( x,x^{\prime} \right) = \left( xx^{\prime} + 1 \right)^{2}$ on $\mathbb{R}$ corresponds to the map $\mathbf{\phi}(x) = \left\lbrack x^{2},\,\sqrt{2}\, x,\, 1 \right\rbrack^{\top}$, and check this for $x = 2$ and $x^{\prime} = - 3$.

**12.7** (a) Show that $k\left( \mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime} \right) = \langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\rangle - 1$ is not a valid kernel. (b) Prove that if $k_{1}$ and $k_{2}$ are valid kernels and $c > 0$, then $k_{1} + k_{2}$ and $c\, k_{1}$ are also valid.

**12.8** For two distinct points at distance $d$ and the RBF kernel with parameter $\gamma$, find the Gram matrix and its eigenvalues, and conclude that this matrix is positive definite. Compute the values for $\gamma = 0.5$ and $d = 2$.

## Conceptual exercises

**12.10** Describe the behaviour of the soft-margin SVM in the two limits $C \rightarrow \infty$ and $C \rightarrow 0$. In each case, what about the number of support vectors and the risk of overfitting or underfitting?

**12.11** With reference to Example 12.7, explain why a very large $\gamma$ in the RBF kernel leads to overfitting, and how this phenomenon appears in Figure 12.15.

**12.12** Why should the features be standardised before training an SVM? Justify your answer using the role of $\| \mathbf{w} \|$ in the objective and the role of distance in the RBF kernel.

**12.13** One can show that the leave-one-out cross-validation error of a hard-margin SVM is at most the ratio of the number of support vectors to the total number of samples. Justify this bound intuitively and compute it for Example 12.2.

## Solutions

**Solution 12.1.** (a) $\| \mathbf{w} \| = 5$. For $\lbrack 4,2\rbrack^{\top}$: $f = 12 + 8 - 10 = 10$, so the label is $+ 1$ and the distance is $10/5 = 2$. For $\lbrack 1,1\rbrack^{\top}$: $f = 3 + 4 - 10 = - 3$, so the label is $- 1$ and the distance is $3/5 = 0.6$. (b) We must divide $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ by $5$ so that the closest point has $|f| = 1$: $\mathbf{w} = \lbrack 0.6,\, 0.8\rbrack^{\top}$ and $b = - 2$. Now $\| \mathbf{w} \| = 1$ and the width of each side of the margin is $1/ \| \mathbf{w} \| = 1$.

**Solution 12.2.** (a) Both points must lie on the margin: $- \left( w_{1} + b \right) = 1$ and $3w_{1} + 4w_{2} + b = 1$. The shortest $\mathbf{w}$ points along $\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{x}_{+} - \mathbf{x}_{-} = \lbrack 2,\, 4\rbrack^{\top}$; with $\mathbf{w} = c\,\mathbf{d}$ and the difference of the two constraints, $c \| \mathbf{d} \|^{2} = 2$ and $c = 2/20 = 0.1$. Hence $\mathbf{w} = \lbrack 0.2,\, 0.4\rbrack^{\top}$, $b = - 1 - 0.2 = - 1.2$, and the margin is $1/ \| \mathbf{w} \| = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$, half the distance between the two points. (b) Any component of $\mathbf{w}$ orthogonal to $\mathbf{d}$ has no effect on the constraints but increases $\| \mathbf{w} \|$; hence $\mathbf{w} = c\,\mathbf{d}$, and the difference of the constraints gives $c = 2/ \| \mathbf{d} \|^{2}$. Adding the two constraints shows that $f$ vanishes at the midpoint; that is, the boundary passes through the middle of the segment and is perpendicular to it.

**Solution 12.3.** From $\sum_{n}^{}\alpha_{n}y_{n} = 0$ we get $\alpha_{+} = \alpha_{-} = \alpha$, and from $\mathbf{w} = \alpha\,\mathbf{d}$ it follows that $\alpha = 0.1$. The dual value (with the maximisation sign) is $\sum_{n}^{}\alpha_{n} - \frac{1}{2} \| \mathbf{w} \|^{2} = 0.2 - 0.1 = 0.1$, and the primal value is $\frac{1}{2} \| \mathbf{w} \|^{2} = \frac{1}{2}(0.04 + 0.16) = 0.1$. The two values are equal.

**Solution 12.4.** The function $\ell$ is the maximum of the two affine functions $0$ and $1 - t$, and the maximum of convex functions is convex. For $t < 1$ the derivative is $- 1$, for $t > 1$ it is $0$, and at $t = 1$ the subderivative is the whole interval $\lbrack - 1,0\rbrack$. By the chain rule with $t = y\left( \langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle + b \right)$, a subgradient with respect to $\mathbf{w}$ is $- y\,\mathbf{x}$ if $t < 1$ and $\mathbf{0}$ if $t \geq 1$; this is exactly (12.54).

**Solution 12.5.** The values of $f$ are $2$, $0.5$, $- 1$ and $0.5$, respectively. Hence $\xi = \lbrack 0,\, 0.5,\, 0,\, 1.5\rbrack$ and the objective is $\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 2 = 5$. Only the fourth point is misclassified ($\xi > 1$). The first point has $yf = 2 > 1$ and cannot be a support vector; the other three have $yf \leq 1$ and can have $\alpha_{n} > 0$ (for the second and fourth points, which have $\xi_{n} > 0$, $\alpha_{n} = C = 2$).

**Solution 12.6.** $\left( xx^{\prime} + 1 \right)^{2} = x^{2}x^{\prime 2} + 2xx^{\prime} + 1 = \langle\left\lbrack x^{2},\sqrt{2}x,1 \right\rbrack,\left\lbrack x^{\prime 2},\sqrt{2}x^{\prime},1 \right\rbrack\rangle$. For $x = 2$ and $x^{\prime} = - 3$: the kernel gives $( - 6 + 1)^{2} = 25$ and the explicit inner product gives $4 \cdot 9 + \left( 2\sqrt{2} \right)\left( - 3\sqrt{2} \right) + 1 = 36 - 12 + 1 = 25$.

**Solution 12.7.** (a) For the single point $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ the Gram matrix is $\lbrack - 1\rbrack$, which is not positive semidefinite. (b) For any set of points and any $\mathbf{z}$ we have $\mathbf{z}^{\top}\left( \mathbf{K}_{1} + \mathbf{K}_{2} \right)\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1}\mathbf{z} + \mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{2}\mathbf{z} \geq 0$ and $\mathbf{z}^{\top}\left( c\mathbf{K}_{1} \right)\mathbf{z} = c\,\mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1}\mathbf{z} \geq 0$; symmetry is also preserved.

**Solution 12.8.** With $\rho = e^{- \gamma d^{2}}$ the Gram matrix is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and its eigenvalues are $1 \pm \rho$. Since $0 < \rho < 1$ for $d > 0$, both eigenvalues are positive. For $\gamma = 0.5$ and $d = 2$: $\rho = e^{- 2} \approx 0.135$ and the eigenvalues are $1.135$ and $0.865$.

**Solution 12.9.** Removing $\mathbf{x}_{6}$ changes nothing, because $\alpha_{6} = 0$ and this point plays no role in $\mathbf{w} = \sum_{n}^{}\alpha_{n}y_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}$. But $\mathbf{x}_{4}$ is a support vector, and removing it can widen the margin and move the boundary. The SVM is sensitive only to the points near the boundary; this is both an advantage (sparsity) and a risk (a single outlier near the boundary changes the solution).

**Solution 12.10.** With $C \rightarrow \infty$ no margin violation is tolerated: the model approaches the hard margin, the margin is narrow, there are few support vectors, and there is a risk of overfitting (and, for non-separable data, of having no solution). With $C \rightarrow 0$ the regulariser dominates: $\mathbf{w} \rightarrow \mathbf{0}$, the margin becomes very wide, almost all points become support vectors, and the model underfits.

**Solution 12.11.** With a large $\gamma$ the kernel is almost zero for any two distinct points and the Gram matrix approaches the identity. Then $f$ is a sum of narrow bumps around the individual training points: the training error becomes zero, but away from the training points $f \approx b$ and no generalisation takes place. In Figure 12.15 this appears as small islands around the points, with almost every sample becoming a support vector.

**Solution 12.12.** The objective penalises $\| \mathbf{w} \|^{2}$, and the weight needed for a feature is inversely proportional to its scale; so a feature with a large scale is "cheaper" and dominates the solution. In the RBF kernel, too, the Euclidean distance is determined by the large-scale features. Standardisation gives all features equal weight.

**Solution 12.13.** If a point that is not a support vector is left out, the solution does not change and that point (which was outside the margin and on the correct side) is classified correctly. So errors can occur only when support vectors are left out, and the number of errors is at most their number. In Example 12.2 this bound is $3/6 = 0.5$ — a loose bound that becomes tight as the data grow while the number of support vectors stays fixed.

**Solution 12.14.** For $C = 1$ you should obtain $\mathbf{w} \approx \lbrack 0.67,\, 0.33\rbrack^{\top}$ and $b \approx - 2.33$ with objective value $3.28$, and for $C = 0.1$, $\mathbf{w} \approx \lbrack 0.42,\, 0.28\rbrack^{\top}$ and $b \approx - 1.77$ with objective value $0.475$. With a constant step, the objective oscillates around the optimum after the initial decrease, with an amplitude proportional to the step size; with a decreasing step the oscillation dies out.

**Solution 12.15.** With $\mathbf{Q} = \left( \mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{\top} \right) \odot \left( \mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top} \right)$ the problem is to minimise $\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\alpha} - \mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{\alpha}$ subject to $\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{\alpha} = 0$ and $\mathbf{\alpha} \geq \mathbf{0}$. The solution is $\mathbf{\alpha} \approx \lbrack 0.111,\, 0.167,\, 0,\, 0.278,\, 0,\, 0\rbrack^{\top}$, which coincides with $\frac{1}{9}$, $\frac{1}{6}$ and $\frac{5}{18}$ of Example 12.5. The complete program is in the book's repository (ch12/dual_qp.py).

**Solution 12.16.** The heat map shows a diagonal band of high accuracy. The corner of small $C$ and small $\gamma$ underfits (accuracy around $0.5$ to $0.8$ and almost all points are support vectors). The corner of large $\gamma$ overfits: training accuracy near $1$, lower validation accuracy, and again many support vectors. The smallest number of support vectors is usually found near the optimal region.

**Solution 12.17.** The two matrices are equal up to rounding error. Computing with the kernel for $N$ points costs on the order of $N^{2}D$, whereas building the features explicitly needs on the order of $ND^{2}$ memory and $N^{2}D^{2}$ time. For $D = 1000$ each feature vector has about half a million components, and the explicit method is hundreds of times slower or does not fit in memory.
